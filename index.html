0 is calm. 10 is overwhelmed.

Kay starts every encounter at 5/10 stress. Get to 0 to win. At 10, the encounter overwhelms Kay—and you can retry with no shame.

Six strategies: slow breathing, small steps, sleep, movement, talking, and asking for help.

A note about stress

Kay is not the enemy.

The alarm is the encounter. Stress is a normal body response, not proof that Kay is bad at coping. The meter is a story device, not a medical measure.

If stress feels constant or overwhelming, talking to a trusted adult or the school counsellor is a real strategy, not a failure.

            Kay's notebook

            Four notes from Kay's health class sit underneath the battle meter. The game's explanations build on them.

            Note A

            Why a presentation

            I chose this scenario because it is the clearest example of the kind of stress my age group actually lives with. All three sources treat stress as something that starts with how the brain interprets a situation, not with the event itself, and a presentation is unusual in that it combines two separate threats at once: academic stakes and public judgement. Unlike an exam, there is nowhere to hide—the stressor is the audience. It also feels familiar to me because at an international school, assessment is constant and quietly competitive, and I have noticed that the week before a presentation I lose sleep and rehearse conversations that never happen. Even now, reading the assignment back, my chest tightens slightly. That physical reaction before anything has actually gone wrong is exactly what the videos describe.

            Note B

            What the alarm does

            The brain checks the room fast. If it sees a threat, the amygdala sends the alarm. Adrenaline makes the heart pound, the breathing go shallow, and the hands shake. Cortisol keeps “me” on edge. The student in my scenario thinks, “Everyone will see me fail.” She feels panic and shame, goes red, and rushes her words. I think, “I've forgotten my line.” I feel dread, get a tight chest, and want to hide or go last. The body is not broken. It is getting ready to fight or run, which does not help when the person has to talk.

            Note C

            Loud alarm, learning brake

            The amygdala, the alarm centre, grows fast in the teen years. The prefrontal cortex, which calms it down, is still being built and will not finish until the mid-twenties. So the alarm is loud and the brake is weak. Teens also care a lot about what others think, so a watching crowd feels like real danger. In the scenario, the teacher says, “You're next.” The student's face goes hot, and her mind empties before she can think. The same happens to me: the first line disappears. That is not weakness. It is a brain still practising how to calm itself.

            Note D

            Grounding, step by step

            Grounding is the coping strategy. Steps: first, stop and feel the feet on the floor. Second, breathe in for four counts and out for six, six to eight times. Third, name five things you can see, three you can hear, and one thing you can feel. Fourth, say to yourself, “I am having a scared thought.” Fifth, walk up and say the first line. Grounding slows the heart and brings attention back to now, so the thinking brain can work again. It is quick, and nobody notices.

            These are Kay's own class notes. The game uses them as the science underneath the story; it is not medical advice, and the stress meter is a story device rather than a clinical measure.

            How to play

            The duel in one sentence

            Bring Kay's stress from 5/10 down to 0 before it reaches 10/10. Every turn you choose one of four drawn moves; then the encounter answers.

            • 0/10 — Calm: the encounter is won. It does not get a counterattack.
            • 10/10 — Maxed: Kay is overwhelmed. This is information about the encounter, not a verdict about Kay. Take a breath and retry.
            • Starting stress: 5/10 in every encounter. Stress resets to 5 for the next encounter.
            • Shuffled hands, fixed effects: the four moves are drawn fresh each turn, but stress changes and counterattacks are never random. No misses, no random damage, no time pressure.

            Four moves, one honest meter

            Each turn offers exactly four moves, numbered 1–4. They are drawn from that encounter's eight-move pool: all six strategies plus two temptations. Effectiveness depends on the encounter: the same coping move can be strong in one moment and neutral in another.

            The draw is fair, not chaotic. The move you just used sits out for one turn, so the same button cannot be spammed. Every hand includes at least one super-effective or effective move and at least one temptation; when Kay has an unlearned strategy, the draw prioritises it. Only which four moves appear is random. Every stress change and counterattack is the fixed number shown in the table.

            Neutral leaves the stress meter exactly where it started: the move and the alarm cancel each other out. No single move can win from 5 stress. A winning move ends the encounter before it can attack. The battle log and the turn feedback show every intermediate number.

            Reading the scene

            Read the encounter's TELL line for context, then check the rating badge on each move button. The badge shows the effectiveness, the move's stress change, the alarm's answer, and the net change for the turn.

            Unhelpful temptations are real options, not traps for the sake of it. If you try one, the game explains why it does not settle the alarm, without shaming Kay.

            Endings and retries

            Retries are tracked, not punished. Losing records a retry, resets stress to 5/10, and keeps every tool and any support progress. At the end, the game checks four routes:

            • Long Night: 3+ retries, the alarm maxed twice, or 9+ stress in two encounters.
            • Open Door: Kay asked a teacher or counsellor for help, with fewer than 3 retries and not a perfect run.
            • Steady Heart: no retries, at least 5 clean wins, and 5–6 tools learned.
            • Good-Enough Morning: Kay won all six, with 1–2 retries or fewer than 5 clean wins.

            A clean win means the alarm never reached 8+ in that encounter.
